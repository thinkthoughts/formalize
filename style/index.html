Style & vocabulary

A living guide for formalize.now: precise writing, readable mathematics, restrained design, and accountable links between human claims, formal statements, and physical evidence. This is a proposed editorial standard, not a new theorem or a replacement for the RLT specification.

1. Purpose and reader

formalize.now is the technical RLT landing page for researchers, theorem-prover developers, software engineers, and careful readers of technical claims. Begin with a comprehensible statement of the problem; provide formal details and evidence through direct links.

Editorial objective

Preserve the writing. Specify its meaning. Check the stated relationship. Identify the evidence, scope, and unresolved conditions.

Do not frame the site as a chronological news publication, awards catalog, generic AI showcase, or a collection of outreach attempts. A completed project belongs here because of what can be read, checked, or tested.

2. Preferred vocabulary

TermUse it forCareful distinction
Reading pointA locally identified written claim, expression, convention, or reference with traceable interpretation.Not automatically a physical observation or a theorem.
RLT (Reading Level Test)The independent first-order proof system and its reading-point/specification work.Do not describe it as simply a Lean plugin or as proving all empirical claims.
Lean 4A proof assistant whose kernel checks proof terms against formal statements in dependent type theory.A successful proof check does not independently validate the informal specification.
ConventionA declared rule for reading notation or written expressions, with a source and scope.Not a measurement or universal physical law.
Formal relationshipA stated derivation or correspondence within a specified logic, including dependencies.Say which definitions and assumptions it uses.
NonfictionWriting intended to make factual claims accountable to available evidence.Label untested interpretations and speculative claims; do not equate publication with verification.
Physical record / measurementObserved, instrumented, or recorded evidence with methods, units, place, and uncertainty where relevant.Logs can document a computational run; they are not automatically environmental measurements.
AuditA bounded examination of statements, proof dependencies, evidence, and omissions.State exactly what was checked and what remains open.
CorrespondenceA documented link between expressions, formal statements, implementations, or observed records.Do not claim the link is proved unless it has a checkable proof.
ConditionalA result depending on named hypotheses.Do not paraphrase as unconditional production readiness.
Origin storyA proposed explanation of how something began.Distinguish claims about origins from present material composition and observation.
Clean datacenterA physical system assessed using explicit energy, emissions, water, and operational boundaries.Never infer environmental performance from software proofs alone.

Further RLT definitions belong in its own specification; this page governs how the website uses them.

3. Write “where” for conditions and locations; “when” for time

Prefer where when naming the setting, domain, or local conditions under which a statement holds. Use when only when timing or a temporal condition is genuinely intended. This is a precision rule, not a blanket grammatical ban.

Reconsider

“When a claim about a physical system has evidence beyond a proof.”

This suggests a temporal event when the intended question is about the evidentiary conditions of a claim.

Prefer

“Where a claim concerns a physical system, what evidence supports its correspondence to observation?”

This names the claim's domain and asks for the relevant evidence.

Use “when” for actual time

“Record when the experiment ran and which instrument was used.”

Other useful distinctions: where a theorem applies; under which assumptions its conclusion follows; at what time a sample was measured. Choose the most precise expression rather than force one word into every sentence.

4. Claims, proof, and evidence

Keep RLT's four layers explicit in substantial audits: convention → written expression → proved relationship → physical record. Link them without conflating them. If a layer has not been established, say so.

Allowed claim statuses

Kernel-checked For exactly identified formal declarations and permitted axioms. Tested For an executed software check with a recorded outcome. Measured For a physical observation with stated method and units. Proposed For work not yet established. Open For an unresolved obligation.

Never turn “the proof checks under assumptions” into “the deployed AI system is safe.” Never treat a measured molecule's physical properties as evidence for an unsupported origin story. Avoid presenting either a zero-origin narrative or its rejection as a substitute for local specifications.

Example — IntelligenceRegulation

“The listed Lean modules passed individual checker replay, and four top-level theorem exports passed nanoda with permitted axioms. Production-security conditions remain to be established.”

Example — carbon dioxide

“A measurement reports CO₂ under a specified sampling method, location, and time. A structural chemical description records one carbon atom bonded to two oxygen atoms per CO₂ molecule.”

The statement about composition is different in kind from claims about the origin or destiny of matter.

6. Homepage and research organization

Primary navigation: RLT → /rlt/; Audits → /rlt-audits/; Research → /#research. The logo returns home. The guide may be linked from the footer as a reference, not added as a fourth primary destination.

Homepage sequence, provisional: technical RLT introduction → Audits → Research → proof/physical correspondence. The order of Audits and Research is an explicit candidate for the next revision, not a fixed rule.

Prefer a concise landing-page description and then a direct project page. Avoid global “Catalog” or “Source” links that unexpectedly lead to unrelated repository collections. Every source link should identify the relevant project, document, or commit.

No visible homepage timestamps: remove edition dates, “latest” badges, and date-sorted stories. Keep dates, commit IDs, pinned toolchains, and run timestamps in research records where they serve reproducibility.

Component guidance

Max content width approximately 1130px; inner padding 25px on desktop, 17px on small screens; body 16px with 1.6 line-height; cards with subtle borders and 12–18px radii; 46px minimum primary-action height. Preserve visible keyboard focus, skip links, reduced-motion support, and mobile reflow.

Use the cyan accent for navigation and calls to action, blue for source links, amber for limited conceptual emphasis, and green for evidence-backed success states. Never use color alone to communicate proof status or completion.

8. Preferred writing patterns

Technical introduction

Model wording

“Lean 4 checks proofs against precisely stated propositions. RLT checks derivations in its own first-order system and organizes the reading points, assumptions, and sources involved in comparing statements.”

Project card

Model structure

Question: what is examined? Established: exactly which result is checked? Boundary: what remains conditional, missing, or unmeasured? Read: a direct project page.

Accurate physical claim

Model wording

“Where a claim concerns observed CO₂, identify the molecular specification, sampling location, measurement method, units, and uncertainty.”

Avoid rhetorical scaffolding

Prefer direct statements to “not X but Y” reversals, generic urgency, vague “breakthrough” language, or language suggesting that a checker proves the correctness of the human specification.

9. Revision and publication rules

Keep this file at /style.html alongside /index.html. Make site-copy changes separately from mathematical claims. Record substantial editorial choices in source control; don't place a public date banner on the homepage.

Before publishing a revision, review: link destinations; exact theorem names and citations; terminology; conditional versus unconditional results; visible text contrast; mobile layout; keyboard access; and whether future sites or measurements are wrongly presented as complete.

Open editorial decisions: whether Research precedes Audits; refined use of “where” in introductory copy; which vocabulary belongs on the technical homepage versus the future general-reader site; shared CSS extraction once multiple pages adopt the same layout.
